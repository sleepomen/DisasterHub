# Debris flows (English translation, draft)

## debris-flow-about｜What a debris flow is and why it is dangerous
- reviewed: no

A debris flow is mud, sand, gravel and boulders mixed with water, flowing downhill under gravity along a slope or channel. In Taiwan it mostly happens on hillsides or in valleys during heavy rain, often at the same time as or shortly after landslides. A debris flow can happen when three conditions are met: a lot of rain, a lot of loose earth and rock, and a steep slope.
Debris flows are fast and hit hard: gravel-type flows move at about 3–10 metres per second and mud-type flows at about 2–20 metres per second. The front is raised and made mostly of huge rocks, with mud following behind. Where a valley opens out, the slope gets gentler and wider and the flow slows down, often spreading into a fan-shaped deposit, so the area around a valley mouth is a danger zone.

## debris-flow-alert｜What yellow and red debris-flow alerts mean
- reviewed: no

Debris-flow and large-scale landslide alerts are issued by the Agency of Rural Development and Soil and Water Conservation (農村水保署) based on Central Weather Administration rainfall data, at two levels:
Yellow alert: rainfall is approaching the alert threshold, and the Central Weather Administration forecasts that heavy rain may continue. Residents should start packing personal belongings and get ready to evacuate, watch for changes around them, follow the latest news and keep the household's lines of contact open; people with limited mobility or who need regular medical treatment are advised to evacuate as a precaution.
Red alert: the rain is clearly heavy and has already reached the alert threshold. Residents should evacuate immediately and go to a safe shelter as arranged by the township office and the local police and fire services.
When necessary, the local government issues an evacuation order to move residents out of the danger zone.

## debris-flow-threshold｜How debris-flow alert thresholds are set
- reviewed: no

Every potential debris-flow stream has its own alert threshold, expressed as accumulated rainfall (millimetres). Thresholds are derived from Central Weather Administration rainfall data and range from 200 mm to 650 mm in steps of 50 mm, 10 levels in all.
Thresholds are normally reviewed once a year. They are reviewed immediately after a major sediment disaster, or when an earthquake has loosened the slopes: for example, areas that had intensity 5-upper can have their threshold lowered by about 1–2 levels, and areas with 6-lower or above by about 2–4 levels, which means that after a big earthquake less rain is needed to reach an alert.
Each stream's threshold can be looked up in the agency's alert threshold table (警戒值簡表).

## debris-flow-signs｜Warning signs before a debris flow
- reviewed: no

Use all five senses:
Hearing: unusual rumbling from the mountain, rocks grinding in the stream, trees cracking, a "go" sound coming from upstream.
Sight: the stream turns unusually muddy, the water rises suddenly, or the flow drops sharply or even stops; driftwood in the water; landslides or debris flows nearby; fire-like light or lightning-like flashes upstream.
Smell: a smell of rotting soil.
Touch: feeling the ground vibrate.
Animals behaving abnormally is also a warning sign.

## debris-flow-before｜Living in a debris-flow risk area: what to prepare
- reviewed: no

Listen to weather reports and to debris-flow and large-scale landslide alerts.
Clear drains to keep water flowing; check that your home is safe and keep doors and windows closed.
Prepare an emergency go-bag, a change of clothes and important documents.
Vulnerable people with limited mobility, and areas that tend to be cut off in heavy rain, should evacuate early.

## debris-flow-evacuate｜How to evacuate from a debris flow
- reviewed: no

Take your emergency go-bag, a change of clothes and important documents and go to an emergency shelter; if the disaster may spread, move on to a shelter further away.
In heavy rain, evacuate to high ground on either side of the stream, and do not go down towards the stream bed; do not cross potential debris-flow streams, and avoid dangerous roads and steep slopes.
After evacuating, tell your relatives and friends which shelter you are in, and leave a note at home saying where you have gone.
