# Earthquakes (English translation, draft)

## earthquake-intensity-scale｜Seismic intensity: levels 0 to 7, 5-lower and 5-upper
- reviewed: no

Intensity is how strongly the ground shakes at one particular place. It is not the same as magnitude: magnitude is the energy an earthquake releases, so one earthquake has a single magnitude but a different intensity in each place.
Taiwan's intensity scale runs from 0 to 7, with levels 5 and 6 each split into lower (弱) and upper (強), giving 10 levels: 0 not felt, 1 micro, 2 very minor, 3 minor, 4 moderate, 5-lower and 5-upper (strong), 6-lower and 6-upper (very strong), 7 severe. This scale has been in use since 1 January 2020 (民國 109 年).
Levels up to 4 are determined by peak ground acceleration (PGA); when the calculated level is 5 or above, peak ground velocity (PGV) is used instead. The indoor descriptions in the scale assume a low floor; shaking is stronger on high floors.

## earthquake-intensity-low｜What intensity 0 to 3 feels like
- reviewed: no

Level 0, not felt: people feel nothing.
Level 1, micro: people at rest or on high floors can feel slight shaking.
Level 2, very minor: most people feel shaking and some sleeping people wake up; hanging objects such as lamps sway slightly; parked cars rock gently, like a lorry passing but very briefly.
Level 3, minor: almost everyone feels shaking and some people are frightened; buildings shake, dishes, doors and windows rattle and hanging objects swing; parked cars rock noticeably and power lines sway slightly.

## earthquake-intensity-mid｜What intensity 4, 5-lower and 5-upper are like
- reviewed: no

Level 4, moderate: considerable fear, some people look for somewhere to take cover, and almost everyone asleep wakes up; buildings shake hard, a few unsecured objects may fall and a few pieces of furniture move, with possible minor damage; tiles may come off a few buildings, small areas of the mountains may have rockfalls, and a very few areas may lose power or water.
5-lower: most people are frightened and find it hard to walk; some unsecured objects fall, a few pieces of furniture may move or tip over, a few doors and windows may be deformed and some walls crack; some mountain areas may have rockfalls, and water, power, gas or communications may be cut in a few areas.
5-upper: almost everyone is frightened and finds it hard to walk; many unsecured objects fall, furniture moves or tips over, some doors and windows are deformed, and a very few buildings with poor earthquake resistance may be damaged or collapse; some mountain areas have rockfalls, soft ground may spout sand and mud, and water, power, gas or communications are cut in some areas.

## earthquake-intensity-high｜What intensity 6-lower, 6-upper and 7 are like
- reviewed: no

6-lower: shaking is so violent it is hard to stand; much furniture moves a long way or tips over, doors and windows are twisted, and some buildings with poor earthquake resistance may be damaged or collapse; some ground cracks, some mountain areas may have landslides, and water, power, gas or communications are cut in some areas.
6-upper: shaking is so violent you cannot stand steadily; besides buildings with poor earthquake resistance, buildings with good resistance may also be damaged; mountain areas may have landslides, and water, power, gas or communications may be cut over wide areas.
Level 7, severe: shaking is so violent you cannot move as you intend; almost all furniture moves a long way or tips over, and some buildings with good earthquake resistance may be damaged or collapse; landslides and ground fissures occur and the landscape may change, water, power, gas or communications are cut over wide areas, and railway tracks bend.

## earthquake-before｜What to prepare before an earthquake
- reviewed: no

Keep a first-aid kit and a fire extinguisher, and make sure your family knows where they are and how to use them.
Know where the main gas, water and electricity switches are and how to turn them off.
Tie down things hanging high up and latch cupboard doors; do not keep heavy objects on high shelves, and fix heavy furniture in place.
Pick a safe place to take cover in your home in advance.
Schools, offices and public places should hold regular drills and plan everyone's role in an emergency.

## earthquake-during-indoor｜Indoors during an earthquake: drop, cover, hold on
- reviewed: no

When the shaking starts, the most important thing is to protect your head and neck: drop, cover and hold on (趴下、掩護、穩住) at once, and do not rush outside.
If there is a table nearby, get under it and hold on to a leg; if the table moves, move with it, so it keeps protecting you. If there is no table, move to a corner, drop and protect your head and neck. In a corner or next to a pillar, watch out for nearby furniture, appliances, lamps, bookshelves or racks, and keep away from windows in case the glass breaks.
Once down, do not move again unless something above you may fall; do not look up, so you are not hit by falling lamps or debris.
At school, get under a desk with your back to the windows, and do not run out of the classroom or up or down stairs; in public places, do not push, and do not use the lift.

## earthquake-during-outdoor｜Outdoors or driving during an earthquake
- reviewed: no

Outdoors is relatively safe; do not run back inside. Protect your head and neck with a bag and wait for the shaking to end. Move away from the sides of buildings, from under trees and from under overpasses, watch for signboards and tiles that may fall from above, and keep away from buildings under construction and from utility poles. In the countryside, keep away from cliff edges, rivers and the sea.
If you are driving, do not brake suddenly: slow down safely, turn on the hazard lights and stop at an open spot by the road (away from buildings, trees and overpasses), and only drive on once you are sure the shaking has stopped.
On a train or the metro, hold a fixed handle or bar and lower your centre of gravity; waiting at a station, drop, cover and hold on where you are, and if it is too crowded to get down, crouch low and protect your head and neck.

## earthquake-after｜What to do after an earthquake
- reviewed: no

First check whether you and your family are hurt, then check on your neighbours and whether they need help.
Turn off gas stoves and heaters and check for gas leaks; if something seems wrong with the gas, open doors and windows gently, leave at once and report it.
Before the water may be cut, store water in buckets, kettles and pots; water in the bathtub can be used to flush the toilet.
Get information about the damage from official sources such as the Central Weather Administration, 全民防災 e 點通 (the national disaster-prevention portal) or the National Fire Agency. Use text messages or messaging apps to tell people you are safe, so phone lines are not jammed.
Wear shoes to avoid cuts from broken glass; use the stairs when leaving a damaged building; keep away from beaches and harbours in case of a tsunami; watch for aftershocks.

## earthquake-after-damaged｜After an earthquake: if the house may be damaged
- reviewed: no

If you hear strange noises or hissing from the building, or new cracks appear in the walls or ceiling or existing cracks grow, first turn off any flames and the electricity, take your emergency go-bag and essentials, and go to the meeting point agreed with your family, such as a nearby park, square, open space or evacuation shelter.
If you are not sure whether gas is leaking, do not switch on any electrical appliances and do not use a lighter for light; use a torch or emergency light instead.
Call out to your family and help them as you leave, and if you can, also help neighbours with limited mobility. In a high-rise, if the fire alarm goes off, go down the stairs to the ground floor and do not use the lift.
When helping someone in a wheelchair, tell them before you move; going downhill or downstairs, turn the wheelchair round and move backwards slowly; avoid lifting the wheelchair, and if you must, do it with three or four people; never separate a person from their mobility aid.

## earthquake-trapped｜Trapped after an earthquake: what to do
- reviewed: no

Stay calm, look around to see what is safe and what is dangerous, work out where you are and wait for rescue.
If you are hurt, stop the bleeding first; if something sharp has gone into you, do not pull it out until proper equipment is available.
Cover your mouth and nose with your hand and breathe slowly to inhale less dust.
If you have your phone, call 119, or ask for help by text message or a messaging app; if the power is out and you cannot charge, save the battery, because calls are hard to get through in the first hours after an earthquake.
If you have a whistle, blow it; if not, knock on a solid wall or metal with something hard so rescuers notice you. Do not keep shouting: it uses up your strength and the sound is easily drowned out by noise.
