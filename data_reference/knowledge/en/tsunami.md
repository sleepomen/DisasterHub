# Tsunamis (English translation, draft)

## tsunami-info-types｜Tsunami warning, tsunami alert and tsunami information: the difference
- reviewed: no

The Central Weather Administration issues four kinds of tsunami information:
Tsunami warning (海嘯警報): an earthquake of magnitude 7.0 or more, shallower than 35 km, occurs off Taiwan; or the Pacific Tsunami Warning Center (PTWC) bulletin, as assessed by the Administration, gives an estimated wave height of grade 1 or above on Taiwan's coast, arriving within 3 hours.
Tsunami alert (海嘯警訊): the PTWC bulletin, as assessed by the Administration, gives an estimated wave height of grade 1 or above on Taiwan's coast, arriving in 3 to 6 hours.
Tsunami information (海嘯消息): issued when the above criteria are not met but the tsunami event may concern the public.
Cancellation of a tsunami warning or alert: issued when the PTWC declares the threat over, or the Administration judges from its observations that the threat is over.
In addition, when an earthquake of magnitude 6.0 or more, shallower than 35 km, occurs off Taiwan, the earthquake report adds the note "coastal areas should guard against sudden changes in sea level"; this is a reminder in the earthquake report, not a tsunami warning and not a tsunami alert.

## tsunami-wave-grades｜Estimated tsunami wave height, grades 0 to 3
- reviewed: no

Grade 0: estimated wave height under 0.3 m; people notice nothing, and buildings and boats are not affected.
Grade 1: 0.3 m or more, under 1 m. People feel a clear push and may be swept away; low buildings are slightly flooded; boats in harbour may be damaged; coastal areas will be hit.
Grade 2: 1 m or more, under 3 m. People will be swept away; lower buildings are submerged; small and medium boats may capsize.
Grade 3: 3 m or more. People will be swept away; buildings are submerged and may be damaged by large floating debris; boats may capsize.

## tsunami-signs｜Warning signs of a tsunami
- reviewed: no

A tsunami usually follows a large earthquake; if you feel a large earthquake by the sea, immediately check whether the government has issued a tsunami warning.
If the sea pulls back quickly and exposes the beach, or waves with white foam come towards the shore from far away, a tsunami is about to arrive: move to high ground at once.

## tsunami-response｜How to evacuate when a tsunami warning is issued
- reviewed: no

When a tsunami warning is issued, follow the evacuation staff's instructions; people near dangerous waters should get ready to leave.
Take water, food and other essentials and head for high ground, ideally the upper floors or roof of a solid building. If there is time, open all doors and windows first to reduce the tsunami's impact on the building.
For short evacuations, avoid driving where possible, so you are not trapped if the tsunami catches you.
If you cannot get to high ground in time, hold on tightly to something fixed, such as a big tree or steel bars; put on a life jacket if you have one, grab hold of something floating or climb onto a large floating object; if you are already in the water, avoid being hit by large floating objects.

## tsunami-after｜Can I go back after the first tsunami wave?
- reviewed: no

No. A second wave may follow the first, so do not go home or to fishing harbours or other waterside places straight away. Stay somewhere safe and only return home after the tsunami warning has been lifted.
Be careful when going back to the affected area or cleaning up at home, and keep away from buildings that are clearly damaged.

## tsunami-about｜Why a tsunami becomes so dangerous near the shore
- reviewed: no

The most common cause of a tsunami is a large, shallow undersea earthquake that shifts the sea floor; the water is lifted and spreads out in all directions. Undersea landslides and undersea volcanic eruptions can also cause tsunamis.
In deep water a tsunami travels at about 700–800 km/h, but its height is only tens of centimetres to one or two metres, so ships hardly notice it. As it nears the shore and the water gets shallower, it slows down and its height grows several times over, which is why it does so much damage on the coast. In water 100 m deep it still travels at about 100 km/h, and in water 10 m deep at about 36 km/h.
